# The Cafeteria Clue: first child playtest

Purpose: find out whether children can investigate independently, understand the evidence, and enjoy discovering something. This is a small formative pilot, not proof of learning effectiveness or launch readiness.

## Session plan

Start with 3–5 children across the proposed ages 8–11, one at a time, with an adult facilitator and a parent or caregiver involved. Include children who enjoy reading and children who prefer action or listening. Plan about 20–25 minutes, with permission to stop sooner. Use anonymous session labels such as P01; no names, accounts, clinical details, audio or video are needed for these notes. Do not send invitations without Julianne's direction.

## Say this first

“We’re trying out a science adventure. We’re testing the game, not you. Some parts may be confusing, and that helps us know what to improve. You can say what you’re thinking, ask for help, take a break or stop whenever you want.”

Then: “Try the adventure as you normally would. Tell me when something interests or confuses you.”

Do not explain the correct answers first or direct the child toward a particular prediction. A prediction that does not match the model is useful learning evidence, not a failed task.

## Observe before helping

Give the child time to look around. If they appear stuck, ask “What are you trying to do?” then “What might you try next?” Point out Help only after they have had a chance to find it. If frustration builds, help promptly; note what help was needed. Record reading assistance separately from hints about science or navigation.

| Moment | What to observe | Neutral follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | Understands the problem, role and first action | “What are you helping Aunt Juju find out?” |
| Clue gathering | Notices the pictures and follows Next without coaching | “What did that clue tell you?” |
| Comparison | Compares both groups rather than only counting one | “How did you decide?” |
| Percentages | Connects 5 of 6 with about 83%; does not treat percentages as extra students | “What does that percentage describe?” |
| Prediction | Chooses an idea without searching for the ‘right’ prediction | “What do you think might happen?” |
| Experiment | Understands why the same food makes a fairer temperature comparison | “What did we change, and what stayed the same?” |
| Reflection | Compares the original prediction with the result | “Did anything surprise you? What do you think now?” |
| Explanation | Uses case evidence and acknowledges uncertainty | “What should the team investigate next, and why?” |
| Optional depth | Finds and chooses whether to read Tell me more | “Was there anything you wanted explained differently?” |
| Finish | Can describe the discovery and expresses curiosity or disinterest | “What would you like to investigate next?” |

## Ask after play

1. “Tell me what you discovered, in your own words.”
2. “What can we say about the salad? What can’t we say yet?”
3. “If a prediction doesn’t match the result, what could a scientist do next?”
4. “What did you enjoy most? Which part felt like work?”
5. “Was there anything you wanted Aunt Juju to say aloud or show you?”
6. “What did you do that a scientist might do in their job?”

Ask the adult separately whether the language and activity feel appropriate. Do not let adult praise or preferences substitute for the child's behavior.

Do not count a skipped optional explanation as failure. Completion speed is not a learning score. Difficulty with percentage arithmetic should be distinguished from misunderstanding which group had more illness.

## Turn observations into decisions

- Fix any blocker, lost progress, inaccessible control or misleading science before another session.
- Prioritize navigation or understanding problems seen in two or more sessions; a single serious issue still warrants action.
- If children finish but cannot explain why the salad deserves investigation, improve the evidence interaction before adding content.
- If reading interrupts interest, test optional narration or more visual interactions rather than removing scientific substance automatically.
- If children understand the group pattern but struggle with percentages, keep counts prominent and improve the percentage explanation.
- If a mismatching prediction feels like failure, revise the guide's language and reflection interaction.
- Preserve the moments children explore voluntarily or want to repeat.

After the pilot, summarize observed evidence, proposed changes and remaining questions. Revise this investigation and try it again before scaling the pattern to other game modes. Keep production deployment as a separate decision.
